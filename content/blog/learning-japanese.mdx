# Learning Japanese: The Three Scripts

Japanese uses three writing systems at once. That's the part that puts people off. Once you know what each script is for, it stops being three separate puzzles and becomes one language with three tools.

![The three Japanese scripts, hiragana, katakana and kanji, shown side by side](/images/content/learningjapanese/learning-japanese.webp)

## Katakana

Bold and angular. Used mostly for foreign words, brand names and emphasis. "Coffee" becomes コーヒー, "pizza" becomes ピザ. If a word sounds borrowed, it's probably Katakana.

## Hiragana

Round and flowing. Used for native words and grammar: verb endings, particles, and anything without a Kanji form. Learn this one first. You can write whole sentences in Hiragana alone.

## Kanji

Characters borrowed from Chinese. Each one carries meaning, not just sound, and a single Kanji can be a whole word. There are thousands, but the roughly 2,000 everyday ones cover almost everything you'll read day to day.

## Which Script Goes Where

| Script | Used for | Example |
|--------|----------|---------|
| Katakana | Foreign words, loanwords, emphasis | コーヒー (coffee) |
| Hiragana | Native words, grammar, verb endings | たべる (to eat) |
| Kanji | Core meaning, nouns, names | 水 (water) |

## The Parts People Skip

### Pitch accent

Japanese doesn't use stress the way English does. It uses pitch, and pitch can flip a word's meaning. 箸 with a high first syllable means chopsticks; 橋 means bridge. Learning it early saves a lot of "but I said it right" arguments with yourself.

### Politeness

The language encodes respect in the grammar itself. -san and -sama change how you address someone, and verb forms shift depending on who you're talking to. It's not decoration on top of the sentence. It's part of it.
